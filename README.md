The website is not a portfolio so much as an archive of evidence. Rather than presenting finished projects in polished frames, it gathers the kinds of objects a life actually leaves behind: receipts, SD cards, polaroids, film strips, business cards, scraps of paper and packaging. These artifacts are not neutral. Each one carries traces of time and use: creases, fingerprints, printer errors, fading ink, the small degradations that turn a generic object into a specific one. The site’s task is not to clean these traces away but to make them legible, to treat them the way a conservator or forensic investigator might treat a piece of material evidence.

On the homepage, this archive appears as a desk left open. A small spread of objects lies in view, not as rigid grid or hero section, but as a loose arrangement of things that have landed near one another by habit and chance. A receipt might overlap a photograph; a film strip could half-obscure a business card. This is the “desk state”: relational, messy in a controlled way, dense with implication but light on explanation. It invites a kind of casual browsing, the way you might idly thumb through what’s on your own table—rather than demanding that the visitor navigate a hierarchy of pages and subpages. At this level, the objects act as signposts into different parts of a life: work, travel, film, games, correspondence, writing. They are metaphors as much as they are records.

Clicking an object, however, does not simply open a new section. Instead, it triggers a shift into an inspection space that owes a debt to survival horror interfaces. In Resident Evil, when a player examines an item, the game pulls that object out of the environment and suspends it in a neutral void, allowing it to hover, rotate, and fill the screen. The staging is overtly artificial; the object floats in impossible space. Yet the model itself is stubbornly material. It is dirty, scratched, worn down, often uncomfortably detailed. There is a productive tension here between unreality of presentation and intimacy of surface. This project borrows that logic. When a visitor inspects an item, the rest of the site recedes and the artifact enters a kind of ritualized spotlight: isolated, enlarged, and made available for close looking. It does not need to obey physical rules of gravity or placement, but its textures should insist that it has been handled, stored, forgotten, rediscovered.

In this inspection state, the object stops being just a navigational badge and becomes a small machine of disclosure. Some artifacts will be straightforward. A scanned postcard turns over to reveal handwriting and a date. A receipt folds open to show line items, location, time. A business card reveals the back side or the imprint of a thumb. Others might hide more elaborate mechanisms: a film strip that, when rotated or clicked, expands into individual frames and links to film notes; an SD card that opens into a catalog of projects, screenshots, process folders; a printed program that, once “unfolded,” reveals annotations, circled titles, or attached memories. The goal is not puzzle for puzzle’s sake, but the sense that objects have layers. That meaning is not just written in captions, but embedded in their physical or procedural behavior. Inspection, then, becomes a small act of investigation: you turn the item in your hands because it might be keeping a secret.

Conceptually, the site sits between two poles: the open desk and the finding aid. A true archival finding aid is rigorous, taxonomic, and complete; it lists boxes, folders, accession numbers, dates, provenance. A desk, by contrast, is provisional and contingent; it reflects whatever is currently in use or recently forgotten. This project wants a little of both. The front-facing experience should feel like stumbling on someone’s working surface, with all the intimacy and partiality that implies. Yet beneath that surface, there is structure: metadata, dates, tags, cross-references, quiet systems that sustain the illusion of casual arrangement. Each object belongs somewhere in a larger map of life—work period, city, relationship, obsession—and the site uses its own hidden cataloging to suggest connections: “related” items that share a time, a person, a place, or a mood.

What distinguishes this archive from a personal brand page is its attitude toward self-presentation. Instead of asserting “this is who I am” through a set of curated highlights, it says “this is what has been left behind.” It is less interested in making a coherent narrative than in preserving the stubborn detail of individual traces. Some artifacts will be glamorous or obviously important; others will be boring, trivial, even slightly embarrassing. All of them, however, are treated with equal seriousness, because all of them belong to the same record. The floating, examinable item is the perfect vehicle for this stance. It brackets off the noise of the web and, for a moment, asks a visitor to look hard at one small, ordinary thing: its wear, its typography, its date stamps, its margins, the way it has aged. In that close looking there is room for memory, for reconsideration, and for the slow construction of a portrait that emerges not from a single statement but from a scattered, evolving archive of objects in space.
