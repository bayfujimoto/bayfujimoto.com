# Desk Objects

## Outline

This document describes the objects on the homepage desk: what they are, how they behave, and why they were chosen. Each object is the entry point to one of the five archival series, plus a sixth object for the Guide. A seventh anomalous object — the amber block — sits apart from the others and remains partially unresolved.

The objects divide into two interaction types: expansions, where clicking an object causes it to separate into component parts that are each independently navigable; and contraptions, where clicking an object initiates a sequence of state changes that must be completed before the series opens. Series with multiple subcollections use the expansion pattern. Series with a single destination use the contraption pattern.

Current object assignments:

- Guide — key on a paper tag (contraption)
- Identity — dossier (expansion into three documents)
- Labor — powder-coated steel strongbox (contraption)
- Consumption — interlocking composite sphere (expansion into five pieces)
- Creation — stone stamp resting on its side next to a paper (contraption)
- Accumulation — string-tied bundle (contraption)
- Amber block — seventh object, purpose unresolved

## The desk as premise

There is a desk. Seen from directly above — the camera high, looking straight down, perspective present but compressed by the angle into something close to flatness. Objects cast shadows. Edges recede slightly toward their far sides. The scene is three-dimensional and reads as a surface. The camera does not move. You are not given a position in space in any conventional sense; you are given only the desk and what rests on it.

This view is not a technical default. It is the site's first argument: that to understand a life through its objects, you must resist the temptation to place yourself inside the scene. The overhead view is the view of the cataloguer, the conservator, the archivist laying out a collection for documentation. Depth is present but subordinated. You see the desk as evidence, not as atmosphere.

The objects do not explain themselves. There is no label at the top of the page naming the sections, no legend, no guide pinned to the wall. A visitor arrives and finds things. What they mean must be inferred from what they are — their material, their scale, their condition, their relationship to one another across the surface. This is the logic of the found collection: meaning is not declared but accumulated, not announced but handled.

The reference here is the item inspection logic of Resident Evil — an unlikely source for an archival interface, and exactly the right one. In those games, objects are isolated from their context, enlarged, turned slowly in the light, examined from angles the story never otherwise permits. The act of inspection is deliberate, unhurried, almost ceremonial. Horror is the vehicle; attention is the mechanism. This site borrows the mechanism without the vehicle. The intensity of that focused attention — the sense that the object in your hands contains something not yet fully known — is redirected toward intimacy rather than dread.

The desk does not represent the archive. It is the archive's first material statement: that a life can be read through what a person keeps, what they reach for, what bears their mark, what they could not bring themselves to throw away.

## The objects

### Guide — key on a paper tag

There is a theory in archival science that every collection has a ghost document — a record that describes all the other records, that stands outside the fonds and accounts for it, that is simultaneously part of the collection and apart from it. The finding aid is this document: a map drawn by the archivist of their own territory.

The Guide is represented by a key.

It sits at a slight remove from the other objects — no label announces its difference, no color distinguishes it. Only its position suggests that it does not quite belong to the same category as the rest. It has a paper tag tied through the bow: the kind of tag attached to stored specimens, unclaimed property, items held in custody pending identification. One face of the tag is visible. It carries something brief — a word, a number, possibly nothing more than a date.

To read the other side, you must turn the key over. This is the interaction in full: a slow rotation, deliberate, the object completing one half-turn in your hands. The reverse face carries the text that names the Guide. Clicking it enters.

A key implies access, custody, the transfer of permission from one hand to another. A key with a tag implies that it was removed from something — a door, a cabinet, a lock — and stored with a note about what it opened. The archive's finding aid is precisely this: a document extracted from the collection it describes, labeled, and left where it can be found. The key does not open a lock in this interface. It opens a document. The distinction is smaller than it appears.

The Resident Evil quality is most unambiguous here. You are holding an object. You do not know what is on the other side. You turn it.

### Identity — dossier

Identity is the series most likely to be approached with practical intent. Biography, CV, contact: the transferable account of a person, the documents that circulate in professional and institutional contexts, the record a stranger reaches for when they want to know who you are in legible terms. It is the series that does the work the rest of the desk does not.

The object is a dossier — a bound document packet, flat on the surface, clasp closed, label present, worn at the corners in the way of things that have been picked up and set down many times. Clicking it loosens the binding; three documents slide out and arrange themselves: a biography card, a CV sheet, a contact slip. Each is independently navigable.

This is the most literal object on the desk. It makes no claim to strangeness, asks for no interpretation, offers its purpose immediately. This is correct. Identity is where a visitor who needs something specific should be able to find it without solving anything. The puzzle logic that governs the other objects would be a cruelty here.

The dossier's risk is precisely its legibility. A folder on a desk is the first image conjured by the phrase "archival interface." What prevents it from collapsing into cliché is not cleverness of form but specificity of material: the particular wear on the cover, the quality of the label's typography, the weight that the clasp implies. A dossier that looks as though it was found in a drawer is different from a dossier that looks as though it was generated for a template. The former carries the history of having been used. The latter carries nothing.

### Labor — powder-coated steel strongbox

Labor arrives on the desk as mass and as silence. A powder-coated steel box — flat-lidded, rectangular, a single central latch on the face — with the specific gravity of things made to last. The reference is the Best Made Co. Type 9 storage box: clean edges, one sturdy metal clasp, a small label on the lid. Utilitarian to the point of being almost military. Indifferent to context.

From directly above, its rectangular silhouette reads immediately and distinctly against everything else on the surface. It occupies space without apologizing for it.

The latch releases. The lid lifts. Inside: the documentation of completed labor — folded drawings, printed specifications, bound reports. Some flat, some rolled. No two alike in form, which is accurate: the projects inside were not alike in scope or context or outcome. Each sheet or packet is independently clickable and opens to a project record.

The strongbox works for Labor because of the distinction it draws between work and the evidence of work. It does not hold tools or instruments — those belong elsewhere, to Creation. It holds the record that work occurred: the contracts, the delivered specifications, the proof of professional exchange. Labor in this archive is the institutional account of effort — work done in relation to other people, other organizations, other structures of accountability. The strongbox is where that account is kept when the engagement is concluded.

One latch, not two. One deliberate release before access. The interaction does not feel like a puzzle. It feels like unlocking a filing cabinet at the end of a long day.

### Consumption — interlocking composite sphere

Emerson wrote — though the exact form varies in retelling — that we do not remember all the meals we have eaten, and yet they have made us. The observation is about the invisibility of influence, the way that what we take in accumulates beneath conscious memory and shapes the self without announcing itself. This is the thesis of the Consumption series, and it is the thesis of its object.

The object is a sphere. Assembled from five interlocking pieces, each carved or cast from a different material — turquoise, dark wood, patinated copper, stone, crystal, or analogues of these. From above, it presents as complete, self-contained, a unified form. The seams between pieces are present but not telegraphed. The sphere does not reveal its own construction.

Clicking it causes the pieces to separate slowly — each sliding or rotating out of its joint, coming to rest on the surface in a loose arrangement. The faces that were hidden inside, the contact surfaces between pieces, are rough and irregular. Crystalline in places. Raw where the exterior was resolved. The complexity that the exterior withheld is now visible.

A sphere carved from a single material speaks of unity of origin. This sphere speaks of something else: five different materials that found their joints over time, from disparate sources, without a prior design that specified how they would fit. The fit is real. It was not inevitable. This is an accurate description of a consumption practice — of the self that emerges from decades of films watched, books read, music absorbed, coffee ritualized, games played. The outside looks like one thing. The inside is five.

The platonic exterior matters precisely because it withholds. A cube has visible faces that imply seams and invite disassembly. A sphere offers no such map. The cuts that divide it into five are non-obvious from any angle. The disassembly, the first time, is a small surprise — which is the correct emotional register for the moment of realizing that a unified thing was always composite.

### Creation — stone stamp and paper

On the desk, beside a sheet of paper — a sketch, a painting, something whose exact nature is deliberately indistinct — lies a stone seal. It is not upright. It rests on its side, as objects rest when they have been set down mid-process, between uses, without ceremony. This is a desk that is being worked at, not a desk arranged for viewing.

The stamp is a personalized seal — the same one pressed into finished artworks, the mark that declares: this is done, this is mine, I was here when it was made. Every object on the desk is, in some sense, a type. A key, a dossier, a strongbox, a bundle — these are categories of object, inhabited by specific instances. The stamp is different. It is this stamp, the one used on finished work. A visitor who has encountered it elsewhere — on the back of a print, in the corner of a drawing, in a photograph of a studio — will recognize it. A visitor who has not will still understand that it belongs to someone particular.

Clicking the stamp causes it to right itself, traverse the paper, and press down. An impression appears. The Creation series opens through the impression, not through the instrument that made it. You enter through what was made.

The resting position is a small argument about the nature of creative practice. Every other object on the desk waits in a neutral state — available, patient, unstarted. The stamp lying on its side has already been used. It is between sessions. The desk is not a display; it is a working surface that you have arrived at in the middle of something.

### Accumulation — string-tied bundle

The bundle is cloth wrapped around things and tied with a piece of string. No wax seal. No clasp. No lock. The string is the only closure, which is to say: the closure is provisional, the kind made not to preserve something in perpetuity but to keep things together while they are being kept.

This distinction was deliberate. An earlier version of this object carried a wax seal — a mark of closure with institutional weight, a decision recorded in material. The seal was removed because the desk already has a mark of authorship: the stone stamp and its impression. Two marks of custody on the same surface would have diluted both. The bundle has no mark. It was gathered and tied because that is what you do with things you cannot bring yourself to discard. The act is not ceremonial. It is habitual.

Clicking it releases the string. The cloth unfolds flat. What was inside is now visible.

What is inside is everything: tickets, receipts, brochures, printed matter, the physical residue of events attended and transactions completed and moments preserved without quite knowing why they should be. The browse that opens is flat and unfiltered. Nothing is organized. The bundle, which implies that someone cared enough to wrap and tie, opens onto accumulation without hierarchy — which is the truth of it. The care was in the keeping, not in the arrangement.

There is a passage in Walter Benjamin's essay on his personal library — the act of unpacking books — about the way that ownership of objects is always the most intimate relationship with the past. The bundle does not contain a library. It contains the residue of a life moving through the world. The intimacy is the same.

## The amber block

Elsewhere on the desk — at a slight remove from the others, without obvious relation to any of them — there is a block of amber resin. Small. Clear, or nearly clear. Objects are visible inside it, distorted by the medium, their precise nature not immediately determinable.

Clicking it sends hairline fractures across the surface. A second click splits the block along these lines, opening like a geode. What is inside becomes accessible.

Its purpose is not resolved. Three possibilities remain open.

It could be the entry point to the administrative layer of the archive — the maintenance interface, accessible to the archivist, not advertised to anyone else. There is a logic to hiding administrative access inside something geological and anomalous. The person who knows what the amber block is will know what to do with it. Everyone else will wonder and move on.

It could be the entry point to a hidden section of the archive: unpublished records, drafts, things that exist in the collection but did not make it into the formal structure. Amber is a preservation medium. Things end up inside it not because they were placed there deliberately but because they were present when the resin hardened. The metaphor is almost too accurate. It may be the right one anyway.

Or it could be inert. An object that rewards examination with nothing. Not everything on a real desk is meaningful, and there is a kind of integrity in an interface that admits this — that includes something that cannot be entered, that resists the assumption that every object is a door.

The amber block and the consumption sphere are related in a way that is not announced. Both are objects whose interiors exceed what the exterior implies. Both involve looking into something rather than opening it in the conventional sense. The sphere was assembled from the outside in; its complexity accumulated. The amber block was sealed from the outside in; its contents were arrested. They belong to the same family of thinking about enclosure, about interiority, about the relationship between a surface and what it contains.

The question of what the amber block is for should be answered last, after the rest of the desk is settled. Its unresolved status is not a problem. It is, for now, the most accurate thing about it.

## The collection as a whole

Considered together, the seven objects on the desk divide into two registers. The first is institutional: the key, the dossier, the strongbox, the bundle. These are objects that could exist in a real archive room, in a real collection. They carry the logic of custody, documentation, official exchange. They are legible without interpretation. The second register is stranger: the composite sphere, the amber block, the stamp lying on its side next to its paper. These objects ask something of the visitor — a willingness to look carefully, to handle without immediately understanding, to allow meaning to arrive slowly.

This is the correct ratio. A desk composed entirely of strange objects would be theatrical, self-conscious, effortful in a way that undermines the intimacy the site is trying to produce. A desk composed entirely of institutional objects would be legible but inert — a diagram rather than a place. The strangeness works because the legibility contains it. The sphere is surprising because the strongbox beside it is not.

The objects vary in scale and overhead silhouette in a way that was not entirely planned but should be preserved: the dossier large and flat, the strongbox medium and rectangular with visible height, the sphere a compact and ambiguous circle, the bundle irregular and organic, the stamp small and elongated beside its sheet of paper, the key the most delicate thing on the surface. From directly above, no two read the same. This matters because the camera does not move. The desk must contain its own visual differentiation.

The deeper question the collection poses — and declines to answer, which is the correct disposition — is whose desk this is. The objects are specific enough to imply a person without describing one. They suggest a set of practices, a history of professional engagement, a habit of keeping things, a mark made repeatedly on finished work, a palate formed over years of intake. A visitor who handles all of it — who turns the key, opens the dossier, unlatches the strongbox, rights the stamp, unties the bundle, and sits for a moment with the amber block in their hands — will have learned something. Not everything. The archive is larger than its surface. But something real, arrived at through objects rather than through description. That is what the desk is for.
