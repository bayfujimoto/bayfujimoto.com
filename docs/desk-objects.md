# Desk Objects

## Outline

This document describes the objects on the homepage desk: what they are, how they behave, and why they were chosen. Each object is the entry point to one of the five archival series, plus a sixth object for the Guide. A seventh anomalous object — the amber block — sits apart from the others and remains partially unresolved.

The objects divide into two interaction types: expansions, where clicking an object causes it to separate into component parts that are each independently navigable; and contraptions, where clicking an object initiates a sequence of state changes that must be completed before the series opens. Series with multiple subcollections use the expansion pattern. Series with a single destination use the contraption pattern.

Current object assignments:

- Guide — key on a paper tag (contraption)
- Identity — dossier (expansion into three documents)
- Labor — powder-coated steel strongbox (contraption)
- Consumption — interlocking composite sphere (expansion into five pieces)
- Creation — stone stamp resting on its side next to a paper (contraption)
- Accumulation — string-tied bundle (contraption)
- Amber block — seventh object, purpose unresolved

## The desk as premise

There is a desk. The camera sits directly above it, looking straight down — perspective present but compressed by the angle into something approaching flatness. Objects cast shadows. Edges recede slightly. The scene is three-dimensional and reads, nonetheless, as a surface. The camera does not move.

This viewing position carries a long disciplinary history. Michel de Certeau, writing about the experience of looking down at Manhattan from the World Trade Center, described the elevated view as one that transforms the city into a text — legible, totalizing, abstracted from the lived experience of the street below. The view from above, he argued, produces a fiction of mastery: the illusion that to see everything at once is to understand it. The archivist's overhead documentation photograph operates in a similar register. Objects laid flat, photographed from directly above, become records of themselves — extracted from context, equalized, made available for comparison and cataloguing.

There is something curious, then, about choosing this view for a personal archive whose stated aim is intimacy rather than mastery. The camera position implies the cataloguer; the contents imply the collected. The tension between these two positions — between the one who arranges and the one who is arranged — is not resolved by the interface. It is, perhaps, the interface's central subject.

The objects on the surface do not explain themselves. There is no legend, no navigation bar, no section headings pinned to the wall. A visitor arrives and finds things. Frances Yates, in *The Art of Memory*, described the classical method of loci as a system in which knowledge was encoded into imagined objects placed in imagined rooms — the practitioner moving through the space, encountering each object, recovering what it stored. The desk does not ask the visitor to move through it; it presents itself all at once. But it shares with the memory palace a certain faith in the object as a vessel — the idea that meaning can be placed inside a thing and retrieved through the act of handling it.

The interaction logic draws from an unexpected source: Resident Evil, the survival horror game franchise, which developed over its thirty-year history an unusually refined vocabulary for object inspection. In those games, items are isolated from their context, enlarged, turned slowly in three-dimensional space, examined from angles the narrative never otherwise permits. The act of inspection is ceremonial, unhurried, almost meditative — a strange formal quality in a genre defined by anxiety. The franchise did not invent the close inspection of objects as a meaningful act; it inherited it from a much longer history of museum display, scientific illustration, and devotional imagery. But it codified that intensity of attention in a form that is now recognizable to a broad audience, which makes it, oddly, a useful reference. The horror is bracketed. The attention remains.

## The objects

### Guide — key on a paper tag

Every archive, in the formulation of archival theory, requires a finding aid: a document that stands outside the fonds and accounts for it, that describes the collection's scope, its provenance, its arrangement, and the principles by which it was organized. The finding aid is simultaneously part of the archive and apart from it — a ghost document, as some theorists have characterized it, that describes all the other documents without quite being one of them.

The Guide is this document. And it is represented by a key.

The key sits at a slight remove from the other objects. No color distinguishes it; no label announces its difference. Only its position on the surface suggests that it does not quite belong to the same category as the rest — a distinction that requires some attention to perceive, which seems appropriate for an object that rewards attention. Tied through the bow is a paper tag of the kind attached to stored specimens, unclaimed property, items held in custody pending identification. One face of the tag is visible. It carries something brief.

To read the other side, you must turn the key over. A slow rotation — the object completing one half-turn, the reverse face coming into view. The reverse carries the text that names the Guide. Clicking it enters.

Jacques Derrida, in *Archive Fever*, proposed that the word "archive" derives from the Greek *arkheion* — the residence of the archons, those who held both the power to keep documents and the power to interpret them. The archive was always, in this etymology, a place of authority as much as a place of storage. The key participates in this history. It implies access, custody, the conditional transfer of permission from one hand to another. A key with a tag implies that it was removed from its lock and stored with a note about what it opened — that the access it represents has been mediated, catalogued, made available through an act of archival decision.

The Resident Evil quality is perhaps most unambiguous here. You are holding an object. You do not know what is on the other side. You turn it.

### Identity — dossier

Identity is the series most likely to be approached with practical intent. Biography, CV, contact: the transferable account of a person, the documents that circulate in professional and institutional contexts, the record a stranger reaches for when they want to understand who you are in legible, portable terms.

The object is a dossier — a bound document packet lying flat on the surface, clasp closed, label present, worn at the corners in the way of things that have been picked up and set down many times. Clicking it loosens the binding; three documents slide out and arrange themselves on the surface: a biography card, a CV sheet, a contact slip. Each is independently navigable.

This is the most literal object on the desk. It makes no strong claim to strangeness, asks for no interpretive effort, offers its purpose without much delay. There are grounds for this. Identity is the series where legibility matters most — where a visitor who needs something specific should be able to find it without first solving a puzzle. The interpretive labor that gives the other objects their character would be, here, a small cruelty.

The dossier's risk is its familiarity. A folder on a desk is among the first images conjured by the phrase "archival interface," and the history of web design is littered with archive metaphors that never quite escaped their own literalness. What might prevent this object from collapsing into that category is not formal invention but material particularity: the specific wear on the cover, the quality of the label's typography, the weight implied by the clasp. Bill Brown, in his work on thing theory, draws a useful distinction between objects — things that remain within their function, that serve their purpose and stay transparent — and things, which are objects that have somehow exceeded their function, that have acquired presence through use or damage or displacement. A dossier that looks designed is an object. A dossier that looks found is, in this sense, becoming a thing. The desk requires the latter.

### Labor — powder-coated steel strongbox

Labor arrives on the desk with mass. A powder-coated steel box — flat-lidded, rectangular, a single central latch on the face — with the specific gravity of objects made for use rather than for looking at. The reference is a Type 9 storage box: clean edges, one sturdy metal clasp, a small label on the lid. Utilitarian to the point of being almost institutional. From directly above, its rectangular silhouette reads immediately, its footprint distinct from everything else on the surface.

The latch releases. The lid lifts. Inside: the documentation of completed labor — folded drawings, printed specifications, bound reports. Some flat, some rolled. No two alike in form, which is accurate to how projects accumulate: heterogeneous in scope, context, and outcome. Each sheet or packet opens to a project record.

One of the more interesting problems in thinking about a personal archive is the question of how to represent work that was done in relation to other people — work that belongs not only to the person who made it but to the institutions, clients, and collaborators who commissioned or received it. The strongbox frames this problem materially. It does not hold instruments for making things; those belong elsewhere, to Creation. It holds the record that work occurred: the deliverables, the specifications, the proof of professional exchange. Labor in this archive is the institutional account of effort — work contextualized by role, by relationship, by the structures of accountability that surround professional life. The strongbox is where that account is kept when the engagement is concluded.

There is something slightly melancholy about a box that holds only the records of finished things. One latch. One deliberate release. The lid opens.

### Consumption — interlocking composite sphere

Emerson, in various formulations across his journals and essays, returned repeatedly to the idea that what we consume shapes us in ways that exceed our memory of consuming it — that the self is composed, in some significant part, of influences that have been absorbed so thoroughly they are no longer distinguishable as influences. The observation has a quality of mild vertigo to it. We are made of things we cannot fully account for.

The object chosen to represent Consumption is a sphere. Assembled from five interlocking pieces, each formed from a different material — turquoise, dark wood, patinated copper, stone, crystal, or analogues of these. From above, it presents as complete, unified, a single resolved form. The seams between pieces are present but not immediately obvious. The sphere does not reveal its own construction.

Clicking it causes the pieces to separate slowly — each sliding or rotating out of its joint, coming to rest on the surface in a loose arrangement. The interior faces, the contact surfaces between pieces, are rough and irregular. Crystalline in places. Raw where the exterior was smooth. The complexity that the exterior withheld is now visible, and the five pieces are each independently navigable.

There is a tradition in material culture studies, articulated by Arjun Appadurai and others, of treating objects not as stable containers of meaning but as entities with social lives — things whose significance shifts as they move between contexts, accumulate histories, change hands. The composite sphere participates in something like this logic, though in a compressed and somewhat inverted form. The five pieces did not travel from different places to find each other; they were always together. But the metaphor of assembly-from-disparate-sources is accurate to the phenomenology of a consumption practice: films watched across decades, books absorbed in different cities, music heard in contexts that no longer exist. The exterior is what that practice looks like from the outside — a coherent self, a legible set of tastes. The interior faces are what it looks like from a vantage point that the exterior never offers.

The platonic exterior matters because it withholds. A cube has visible faces that imply seams. A sphere offers no map of its own division. The cuts that separate the five pieces are, ideally, non-obvious from any angle — so that the disassembly, the first time, produces a small surprise. That register of mild, considered surprise seems like the correct one for the moment of recognizing that a unified thing was always, underneath, composite.

### Creation — stone stamp and paper

Beside a piece of paper — a sketch, a painting, something whose precise nature is left deliberately indistinct — lies a stone seal, resting on its side. It is not upright and ready. It is resting, as objects rest when they have been set down between uses, without ceremony. The desk is a working surface, not a display.

The stamp is a personalized seal, the same one pressed into finished artworks — the mark of completion, the declaration that a thing has been made and is done and belongs to someone. Most objects on the desk are types inhabited by instances: a key, a dossier, a strongbox, a bundle. The stamp is closer to an instance that has become its own type through use. A visitor who has encountered it elsewhere — on the back of a print, in the corner of a drawing, in a photograph of a studio — will recognize it. A visitor who has not will still understand, perhaps, that it belongs to someone particular.

Georg Simmel, in his short essay on the handle, observed that the decorative handle of a vase occupies a curious position: it belongs to the world of practical objects while also belonging to the world of aesthetic objects, mediating between the thing-as-used and the thing-as-contemplated. The stamp, lying on its side, is in some sense between these two states. It is not being used. It is not purely on display. It is resting in the interval between one act of making and the next.

Clicking the stamp causes it to right itself, traverse the paper, and press down. An impression appears. The Creation series opens through the impression — through what was made — rather than through the instrument that made it.

### Accumulation — string-tied bundle

The bundle is cloth wrapped around things and tied with a piece of string. No seal, no clasp, no lock. The string is the only closure, which is to say: the closure is provisional, of the kind made not to preserve something against the world but to keep things together while they are being kept.

An earlier version of this object included a wax seal — a mark of closure with institutional weight, a decision recorded in material. The seal was removed. The desk already has a mark: the stamp and its impression. Two marks of authorship or custody on the same surface would have diluted both. The bundle now has no mark. It was gathered and tied because that is what one does with things one cannot bring oneself to discard. The act carries no ceremony.

Susan Stewart, in *On Longing*, distinguishes between the souvenir and the collection. The souvenir, she argues, is about the authenticity of personal experience — it marks a moment of origin and belongs to the individual body that was there. The collection, by contrast, creates a series and claims a kind of completeness. The bundle sits uneasily between these categories. It is too heterogeneous to be a collection in Stewart's sense — no organizing logic governs what is inside — and yet it is more than a single souvenir, a single moment. It is accumulated souvenirs, which may be a category that resists both of her terms. Things kept without always knowing why. Things that survived because they were put somewhere and not thrown away.

Clicking it releases the string. The cloth unfolds flat. What was inside is now visible: tickets, receipts, brochures, printed matter, the physical residue of events attended and transactions completed and moments preserved without a clear decision to preserve them. The browse that opens is flat and unfiltered. The bundle implies, by its form, that someone cared enough to wrap and tie. The contents resist this implication.

## The amber block

Elsewhere on the desk — at a slight remove from the others, without obvious relation to any of them — there is a block of amber resin. Small. Clear, or nearly clear. Objects are visible inside it, their precise nature not immediately determinable, distorted slightly by the medium that holds them.

Amber is, among other things, a preservation technology. It has preserved specimens of Cretaceous-era organisms in sufficient condition for molecular analysis — an archival fidelity that far exceeds anything human institutions have managed to achieve. What amber does not offer is access. The contents are visible but not reachable. The preservation and the inaccessibility are the same thing.

Clicking it sends hairline fractures across the surface. A second click splits the block along these lines, opening like a geode. What is inside becomes accessible.

Its purpose is not resolved, and the unresolvedness feels, at this stage, worth sitting with rather than closing off.

It could be the entry point to the administrative layer of the archive — the maintenance interface, accessible to the archivist and not advertised to anyone else. Derrida's archon, who held both the power to store and the power to interpret, kept their documents in a residence with a door. The amber block may be that door, identifiable only to those who already know what they are looking for.

It could be the entry point to a hidden or off-record section of the archive: unpublished records, drafts, things that were made but did not become part of the formal structure. There is something in the amber metaphor — things present at the moment the resin hardened, preserved not by intention but by proximity — that resonates with how drafts and discarded work tend to accumulate. Not placed inside deliberately, but there.

Or it could be inert. An object that rewards examination with nothing that can be entered or used. Not everything on a real desk is meaningful, and there is a kind of intellectual honesty in an interface that acknowledges this — that includes something that simply is, that does not resolve into navigation or content.

The amber block also stands in a quiet formal relationship with the composite sphere. Both are objects whose interiors exceed what their exteriors imply. Both involve looking into something rather than opening it in the conventional sense. The sphere was assembled gradually, its complexity accumulated from outside in; the amber block was sealed, its contents arrested. They do not explain each other. They belong, perhaps, to the same underlying preoccupation with enclosure — with the relationship between a surface and what it contains, between visibility and access.

The question of what the amber block is for should be answered last, after the rest of the desk is settled. Its ambiguity is, for now, the most accurate thing about it.

## The collection as a whole

Considered together, the seven objects divide into two loose registers, though the division is not clean and the interest lies partly in where it fails to hold.

The first register is institutional: the key, the dossier, the strongbox, the bundle. These are objects that might plausibly exist in a real archive room — objects that carry the logic of custody, of documentation, of official exchange. They are legible without much interpretive effort. The second register is stranger: the composite sphere, the amber block, the stamp lying on its side beside its paper. These objects ask something of the visitor — a willingness to look carefully, to handle without immediately understanding, to allow meaning to arrive at its own pace.

This ratio seems about right, though it is worth acknowledging that the division is somewhat arbitrary. The strongbox is strange in its own way — a steel box on a personal desk suggests something being kept under conditions that exceed what the contents require. The key is institutional and also deeply personal, since a key implies a specific lock that only this key fits. The stamp is specific in a way that the other objects are not, and yet stamping is one of the oldest institutional gestures in the record. The registers bleed into each other, which is probably the honest condition of a personal archive: simultaneously intimate and bureaucratic, simultaneously private and intended to be found.

The objects vary in scale and overhead silhouette in ways that were not entirely planned but seem worth preserving. The dossier is large and flat. The strongbox is medium and rectangular with visible height. The sphere is a compact, ambiguous circle. The bundle is irregular and organic. The stamp is small and elongated beside its paper. The key is the most delicate thing on the surface. From directly above, no two read the same, which matters because the camera does not move and the desk must contain its own differentiation.

The deeper question the collection poses — and quietly declines to answer — is whose desk this is. The objects are specific enough to imply a person without quite describing one. They suggest a set of practices: a history of professional engagement, a habit of keeping things, a mark made repeatedly on finished work, a palate formed across years of intake, a record of effort stored and sealed. A visitor who handles all of it — who turns the key, opens the dossier, unlatches the strongbox, rights the stamp, unties the bundle — will have arrived at something. Not a biography. Not a complete account. Something more like what Walter Benjamin described in his essay on unpacking his library: the relationship between a person and their objects as a disorder that contains its own order, legible from inside but resistant to summary from outside. The desk does not describe a life. It constitutes one of the conditions under which a life might be read.
