# Desk Objects

## Outline

This document describes the objects on the homepage desk: what they are, how they behave, and why they were chosen. Each object is the entry point to one of the five archival series, plus a sixth object for the Guide. A seventh anomalous object — the amber block — sits apart from the others and remains partially unresolved.

The objects divide into two interaction types: expansions, where clicking an object causes it to separate into component parts that are each independently navigable; and contraptions, where clicking an object initiates a sequence of state changes that must be completed before the series opens. Series with multiple subcollections use the expansion pattern. Series with a single destination use the contraption pattern.

Current object assignments:

- Guide — key on a paper tag (contraption)
- Identity — dossier (expansion into three documents)
- Labor — TBD (contraption)
- Consumption — interlocking composite sphere (expansion into five pieces)
- Creation — stone stamp (contraption)
- Accumulation — wax-sealed bundle (contraption)
- Amber block — seventh object, purpose unresolved

---

## The desk as premise

The homepage is a desk seen from directly above. The camera is orthographic — no perspective distortion, no vanishing point. Everything is equidistant, equally legible, equally present. The objects rest on the surface and the surface is the only context they are given.

This is a deliberate constraint. A conventional portfolio names its sections in a navigation bar and lets the visitor decide where to go. This site offers no such map at the top level. The objects are the navigation, and the objects do not explain themselves. A visitor arriving at the desk for the first time sees a collection of things and must decide what they mean and what to do with them.

This approach borrows directly from the item inspection logic of Resident Evil: objects are isolated, enlarged, examined carefully, and accompanied by contextual information only after engagement. The difference is tone. Resident Evil uses that intensity of inspection to build dread. This site uses it to build intimacy — the sense that you are handling something real, that the objects were here before you arrived, that they belong to someone.

The desk is not a metaphor layered on top of the content. It is the content's first statement: that a life can be described through what a person keeps on their desk, what they reach for, what they've accumulated, what bears their mark.

## The objects

### Guide — key on a paper tag

The Guide is the archive's finding aid: its map, its philosophy, its description of itself. It is not a series. It is the archive's account of its own existence, and it is represented by a key.

The key sits slightly apart from the other objects, which is the only visual signal of its difference. It has a paper tag tied through the bow — the kind of tag used on storage boxes, labeled specimens, or items held in evidence. One side of the tag faces up. It says something brief: "Guide" or "Archive" or possibly nothing more than a record number.

Clicking the key initiates a rotation — a slow, deliberate turn that brings the reverse side of the tag into view. This is the interaction in full: you turn the object over to read what's on the back. The reverse side carries the text that names the guide. Clicking the reverse face enters the series.

This object is the strongest on the desk because it is the simplest. A key implies access. A key with a tag implies that it was labeled by someone, stored somewhere, and is now available to be used. The archive guide is precisely that: a document that says here is what this place is, here is how to use it, here is what it contains. A key with a tag on a desk, waiting.

The Resident Evil quality is most legible here. The rotation is unhurried. You are examining an object. The information is on the other side.

### Identity — dossier

Identity contains the series most likely to be navigated with practical intent: biography, CV, contact. These are the sections a visitor reaches for when they want to know who you are in legible, transferable terms.

The object is a dossier — a bound document packet lying flat on the desk. It has a clasp, a label, visible wear. Clicking it causes the binding to loosen and three documents to slide out and arrange themselves on the desk surface: a biography card, a CV sheet, a contact slip. Each is independently clickable and navigates to its subcollection.

This is the most literal object on the desk, and that is the right call. Identity is the series where legibility matters most. A visitor who needs to read a CV or find contact information should not have to solve a puzzle to get there. The dossier reads immediately as a document container, which is what it is.

The risk of the dossier is that it is also the most expected object in any archive-themed interface — a folder on a desk is the default image of this aesthetic. What prevents it from reading as generic is material specificity: the wear on the cover, the weight implied by the clasp, the particular quality of the label. A dossier that looks found is different from a dossier that looks designed. The former belongs on this desk; the latter belongs on a template.

### Labor — TBD

Labor is the series for professional work, projects, and effort. It has no subcollections and navigates to a single browse interior. The object should be a contraption — something that goes through a state sequence before opening — and it should not be paper-based.

No object has been confirmed for this series yet. The constraint is that the stamp, which was initially considered for Labor, was reassigned to Creation. Whatever sits in this position needs to carry a sense of work and process without overlapping with the mark-making quality of the stamp, the document quality of the dossier, or the assembly quality of the consumption object. This slot remains open.

### Consumption — interlocking composite sphere

Consumption is the archive's record of intake: films watched, books read, music listened to, coffee brewed, games played. It is the series that is most clearly about accumulation over time, about the slow formation of taste and habit, about what you have taken in without always knowing you were being changed by it.

The object is a sphere assembled from five interlocking pieces, each carved from a different material — turquoise, dark wood, patinated copper, stone, crystal, or similar. From above, the assembled form appears complete and self-contained. The seams between pieces are present but not immediately obvious. The sphere reads as a single unified object.

Clicking it causes the pieces to separate slowly, each sliding or rotating out of its joint and coming to rest on the desk surface in a loose arrangement. The faces that were hidden inside — the contact surfaces between pieces — are rough and irregular, crystalline in places, raw where the exterior was smooth. Each piece is independently clickable and navigates to one subcollection of the Consumption series.

This object is grounded in an Emerson observation: that we do not remember all the books we have read or the meals we have eaten, and yet they have made us. The sphere's exterior is what consumption looks like from the outside — a coherent, resolved whole. The interior faces are what it looks like from the inside: five different materials that don't obviously belong together, that formed their joints over time rather than by design. The complexity was always there. You had to take it apart to see it.

The mismatched materials are doing significant conceptual work. A sphere carved from a single material would suggest unity of origin. A sphere assembled from five different materials suggests that the whole was composed gradually, from disparate sources, and that the fit is real but not inevitable. This is an accurate description of a consumption practice.

The platonic exterior — a sphere rather than a more complex form — matters because it withholds information. A cube has visible faces that imply seams. A sphere doesn't telegraph its own divisions. The cuts that separate the five pieces are non-obvious from above, which means the disassembly is genuinely surprising the first time.

### Creation — stone stamp

The stamp is a personalized stone seal — the same stamp used to mark finished artworks. It sits on the desk handle-up.

Clicking it causes the stamp to lift and press down onto the desk surface. An impression appears. The creation series opens through what was just made.

The reassignment of the stamp from Labor to Creation was the right correction. A stamp is a mark-making tool, and the act of stamping is the act of authoring — of declaring that something is finished, that it bears your mark, that you made it. Labor is about work and effort as process. Creation is about the things that resulted. The stamp belongs with the results.

There is also a biographical specificity to this object that no other object on the desk has in the same way. Every other object is a type — a key, a dossier, a sphere, a bundle. The stamp is this particular stamp, the one used on finished work. A visitor who recognizes it from elsewhere — from the back of a print, from a studio photograph — will feel something specific when they see it on the desk. A visitor who doesn't recognize it will still understand that it belongs to someone.

The impression mechanic deserves careful thought. The question of whether the impression or the stamp is the clickable thing matters. If the stamp itself is clicked and held as the entry point, the impression is an effect — a consequence of the interaction. If the impression becomes the clickable thing after it appears, the entry point is the mark rather than the tool. The latter has a stronger conceptual argument: you enter Creation through what was made, not through the instrument that made it.

### Accumulation — wax-sealed bundle

Accumulation is the archive of physical ephemera: tickets, receipts, brochures, handouts, printed matter. Things kept without always knowing why. Things that survived because they were put somewhere and not thrown away.

The object is a cloth-wrapped bundle tied with twine and sealed with a wax stamp. It sits on the desk as a closed, opaque form. You cannot see what is inside. The seal is the only indication that someone, at some point, decided this should stay closed.

Clicking it initiates a three-state sequence: the wax seal cracks, the twine loosens and falls away, and the cloth unfolds flat to reveal the ephemera inside. The interaction is slow and deliberate. You are opening something that was sealed. The sequence acknowledges the act of opening — it doesn't skip to the contents.

The wax seal connects the bundle to the stamp on the other side of the desk, though the connection is not explicit. Both are marks of completion or custody. The stamp says: I made this. The wax seal says: this was closed, kept, handed forward. Together they bracket the archive's two modes of relationship to objects — the things you made and the things you accumulated.

The bundle is the most archival object on the desk in a conventional sense. It could exist in a real archive room, in a real collection. It has no strangeness. What gives it tension is the three-state sequence — the deliberateness of the unsealing — and the question of what is inside. The browse view behind the bundle is flat and unfiltered. Everything goes in together. The bundle, which implies careful curation, opens onto accumulation without hierarchy.

## The amber block

The amber block is a small piece of clear resin with objects faintly visible inside. It does not obviously belong to any series. It sits on the desk at a slight remove from the other objects.

Clicking it causes hairline cracks to spread across the surface. A second click splits the block cleanly along its fracture lines, opening like a geode. What is inside becomes accessible.

The amber block's purpose is unresolved. Three possibilities have been identified. It could be the entry point to the admin interface — the maintenance layer of the archive, accessible to the archivist but not advertised to visitors. There is something appropriate about administrative access being hidden inside a geological anomaly on the desk, something that requires knowing to look for it. It could be the entry point to a hidden or off-record section of the archive: unpublished items, drafts, things that didn't make it into the formal structure. Amber preserves things that were not meant to be seen again, which is an almost too-accurate metaphor for that purpose. Or it could be inert — an object on the desk that can be inspected but not entered, that does nothing, that has no function. Not everything on a real desk is meaningful, and the restraint of an object that rewards curiosity with nothing is its own kind of statement.

The amber block also creates a visual and material relationship with the consumption sphere. Both are objects whose interiors are more complex than their exteriors suggest. Both involve looking into something rather than opening it. The sphere disassembles; the amber block fractures. The sphere's complexity was assembled over time; the amber's contents were trapped. They are not the same object, but they belong to the same family of thinking.

The decision about what the amber block is for should be made carefully and late — after the rest of the desk is settled. Its ambiguity is currently an asset.

## The collection as a whole

The desk currently has a coherent archival core — the key, the dossier, the stamp, the bundle — and two objects that depart from it in interesting ways: the consumption sphere and the amber block. The figurine logic of the sphere (five mismatched materials assembled into a unified form) and the geological logic of the amber block (things preserved inside something that looks solid) give the desk two moments of genuine strangeness against a background of institutional legibility.

The site concept asks the desk to feel archival, intimate, specific, and restrained. It asks it not to feel game-like or theatrical. The current collection navigates that balance reasonably well. The key, dossier, stamp, and bundle are grounded. The sphere is strange but coherent. The amber block is strange without being theatrical.

The outstanding risk is Labor, whose object is unconfirmed. A weak or mismatched object in that slot will be visible against the strength of the others. Whatever goes there should belong to the same sensibility: specific, slightly worn, legible in purpose but not in detail. Something that could have been on this desk before the archive existed.

The deeper question the collection asks — and does not answer, which is correct — is whose desk this is. The objects are specific enough to imply a person without describing one. A visitor handles the key, opens the dossier, lifts the stamp, unseals the bundle, and gradually builds a picture from material evidence. That is the site's premise. The desk makes it literal.
