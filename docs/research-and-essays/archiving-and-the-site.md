# The Represented Object: Archival Principles and This Site

*Design philosophy essay — compiled April 2026*

---

## The Problem of Representation

A scan of a ticket stub is not a ticket stub. This should be obvious, and yet it is a distinction that slips away easily when building a digital archive — especially one whose design language borrows heavily from physical containers, material surfaces, and the tactile logic of desks and drawers and binders. The scan is a representation. It carries information about the ticket: its dimensions, its printing, its date, its wear. It does not carry the ticket's weight, its smell of old paper, the fold it acquired in someone's pocket. The representation is also, inevitably, an interpretation — a framing, a cropping, a resolution, a color space, a file format.

This is the central tension of this project, and it is the tension that runs through all digital archiving: how to preserve or communicate the materiality of an object in a medium that is categorically not that object. The institutional archive at least has the object. The digital archive has only its image.

This essay thinks through how that tension shapes the decisions behind this site — and how the principles of archival practice, documented in `docs/research-archiving.md`, clarify what the site is trying to do and where it is honest about what it cannot.

## The Desk as Arkheion

Derrida locates the origin of the archive in the *arkheion* — the house of the archons, where both the records and the authority over them resided together. The archive names a jurisdiction as much as a collection. To archive something is to bring it under a kind of law.

The desk is this project's *arkheion*. It is where the archive announces its scope. Five objects on a surface: not a menu, not a grid of thumbnails, not a list of categories — objects. Each object represents a series: a way that material enters or leaves a life. Identity. Work. Consumption. Creation. Accumulation. The desk does not describe what is in these series. It declares their existence, their separateness, their equivalence as top-level containers. The desk is an authority structure before it is a navigation system.

The choice of five series is, in archival terms, an act of appraisal. Every item in this archive will be assigned to one of these five fonds, and the fonds were not discovered — they were decided. Schellenberg's criteria for appraisal (uniqueness, form, significance) apply oddly here, because the archivist is the record creator. The appraisal is self-directed: what domains of a life are worth treating as distinct archival series? The answer — identity, labor, consumption, making, accumulation — is a theory about what constitutes a life, or at least this one.

The five series are not neutral. They reflect a particular way of organizing experience. *Consumption* is a category only if what you take in (films, books, coffee, influences) is treated as evidential — as something that leaves traces, that constitutes a record of attention and taste over time. *Accumulation* is a category only if the residual, the ephemeral, the paperwork of ordinary life is treated as worth keeping rather than discarding. The decision to make these full series — equal in status to *Work* and *Identity* — is itself an argument about archival value. It says: intake is as constitutive of a life as output. Receipts are as significant as case studies.

This is a Jenkinsonian impulse in one sense — the archive should reflect what actually accumulated, not only what was thought significant in advance. But it is a Schellenbergian act in another — the decision to keep, and the decision about how to group, required active appraisal.

## Respect des Fonds, Turned Inward

The principle of *respect des fonds* holds that records from different provenance should not be intermingled. A personal archive complicates this doctrine because the entire archive shares a single provenance: the person. Every item in every series was generated by, or passed through, the same life. The fonds is indivisible at the person level.

What *respect des fonds* offers, once turned inward to a personal collection, is not a rule about keeping collections separate but a logic of coherence: the archive should preserve the organic relations between items. A film ticket and the coffee bag from the same week and the sketch drawn on that afternoon should be capable of being associated — not because they are topically related, but because they share a temporal and experiential origin. The relationship metadata in the content model (`linked_event`, `companion_to`, `watched_with`, `linked_place`) is this principle made concrete. The system should be able to reconstruct proximity, to say: these things existed in the same moment.

*Original order* is even more difficult to preserve in a personal digital archive. The original order of a box of ephemera is the order in which things were placed in it — the bottom of the box is older; things fall against other things. This is actual evidence. Translating it into a digital browse system inevitably imposes a retrospective organization: sorted by date, filtered by type, grouped by year. The site's browse layers (log-list, contact-sheet, project-grid) sort chronologically by `sort_date`, which is the most principled available approximation of original order, but it is an approximation. The actual sequence in which items were acquired, saved, or noticed is partially lost.

This is not a failure. It is an honest concession. The rendering strategy documentation (`docs/rendering-strategy.md`) states a preference for clarity over simulation at the browse layer. But it is worth naming what that clarity costs: a flattening of the organic, slightly chaotic sequence in which things actually accumulate. The archive guide and the item-level `context_note` fields are the site's acknowledgment of this — places where the logic of accumulation, rather than just its content, can be partially restored.

## The Hierarchy of Material Fidelity

The rendering strategy (`docs/rendering-strategy.md`) defines a four-level hierarchy: plain HTML/CSS, image-based 2D, pseudo-3D, actual 3D. This hierarchy is usually read as a production decision — how much technical complexity to invest at each layer. But it is also, read through the lens of archival theory, a statement about representation and fidelity.

Plain HTML/CSS is the most honest medium for metadata: text is text, labels are labels, a date is a date. There is no pretense of material presence. The browse systems, the timelines, the record grids, the captions — these live in HTML because they are information, and information should be legible as information. The moment a date is rendered as a smeared ink stamp on fake aged paper, it becomes harder to read and easier to misdate. Legibility is a preservation value.

Image-based 2D is where representation begins. A scan renders the surface of a document: its ink, its paper, its aging. The scan makes claims about the physical object that it cannot fully substantiate — the color of the scan is calibrated to the scanner, not to the document under controlled archival lighting; the texture visible in a high-resolution scan is the grain of the scanning bed as much as the grain of the paper. But this representation is honest in its framing: it is explicitly an image. You can zoom into it, see the pixels resolve. It does not pretend to be the thing.

Pseudo-3D is where the project begins to make deliberate aesthetic claims. A binder that tilts and catches light; a ledger whose edge tabs show their layering; a drawer whose depth is suggested by shadow and parallax — these are not assertions of physical presence, they are evocations of it. The decision to use pseudo-3D for the category interiors (the binder for Work, the ledger for Consumption, the flat file for Accumulation) acknowledges that material metaphor has value as orientation — it tells the user what kind of container they are handling — while stopping short of simulation.

Actual 3D appears, per confirmed decisions (`docs/decisions.md`), only on the homepage and for a small set of selected inspectable items. It is the most materially ambitious rendering mode and the most fragile: 3D models can degrade, libraries change, mobile performance varies. The decision to use it selectively — "only when actual spatial understanding adds value" — is a version of the archivist's principle of proportionality: the level of treatment should match the significance and accessibility needs of the material.

What this hierarchy describes, in aggregate, is a gradient from information to presence — from the unambiguously digital to the vaguely physical. The site is honest about where it sits on that gradient at each layer. It does not try to make everything feel physical, because that would be both technically unsustainable and epistemically dishonest. The scan of a ticket is not the ticket. But you can see it clearly, zoom into its printing, read its date, and access the notes someone wrote about the night it represents. That is a different kind of preservation than the institutional archive offers — and a different kind of value.

## The Finding Aid as Interface

The finding aid in institutional archival practice is a document that describes a collection: its provenance, its arrangement, its scope, its contents, its level of description. It exists to orient the researcher before they encounter the material. It is an access tool.

This site's navigation system is a distributed finding aid. The desk is the collection-level description: five series, one archive, one life. The category interior is the series-level description: what kind of material lives here, organized as what kind of physical container. The browse view is the file and item-level description: titles, dates, types, statuses, sortable by multiple criteria. The inspection panel is the full item record: all fields, all assets, all notes, all related items.

The multi-level description principle from ISAD(G) — that description at each level applies to everything below it — is built into the URL and data structure. An item at `/accumulation/ephemera/?item=EPH-2025-041` inherits the context of the Accumulation series and the ephemera subcollection before the item record adds its own specificity. The breadcrumb trail (`← desk / accumulation / ephemera`) is not just navigation — it is a constantly visible position statement in the archival hierarchy.

The minimum metadata requirements in `docs/content-model.md` (id, slug, title, series, subcollection, item_type, status, display_date) map directly onto the ISAD(G) required elements. The insistence on stable IDs and slugs — never recycled, always unique — reflects the archivist's commitment to persistent identification: a record, once assigned an identifier, should be findable by that identifier indefinitely. The `context_note` field is the item-level scope-and-content note, the place where meaning beyond the metadata can be written.

## Ephemera and the Accumulation Series

The Accumulation series is the site's most archivally interesting category, and probably its most theoretically honest. It exists to preserve what was never meant to be preserved: brochures, tickets, receipts, handouts, printed matter. These items were not made to last. They arrived in a life as byproducts of activity — admissions, purchases, events, encounters. They are traces, residues. They survived because someone, in some moment of counter-instinct, kept them.

In physical archival practice, ephemera require particular care precisely because their materials are often the least durable. Acidic newsprint, fugitive inks, brittle adhesives — the ephemeron is materially fragile in proportion to its intentional disposability. The preservation of ephemera in institutional collections is an argument about cultural value: that the printed matter of everyday life, not designed to be significant, becomes significant in aggregate, in sequence, across time.

The same argument underlies the Accumulation series. A single museum brochure is not significant. A collection of them, with dates and places and contextual notes, begins to describe a pattern of attention — where the archive's creator was, what they chose to enter, what their leisure looked like, how institutions of culture presented themselves to a visitor across years. The brochure from 2019 and the brochure from 2025 are individually trivial. Together they are evidence of a continuous, evolving relationship with cultural life.

The inspection model is most meaningful for ephemera. The front/back toggle — the ability to see the print on both sides of a ticket, to read what was stamped on the back of a receipt — is the digital equivalent of turning an object over. The zoom and detail view are the equivalent of bringing it under a loupe. The inspection state, influenced by the Resident Evil item-examination mechanic (cited in `docs/site-concept.md`), is about focused attention: the item is isolated, enlarged, examined with care. This is how archivists handle fragile material. It is how an object is read rather than just seen.

The ritual of inspection does something important: it communicates that the item matters. The intensity of focus, the dimming of the browse view behind, the isolation of the object — these are not just aesthetic choices. They are arguments about significance. They say: this thing, which was made to be thrown away, is worth examining carefully.

## What the Site Cannot Do

Institutional archives hold original objects. This site does not and cannot. The gap between the institutional archive and the personal digital archive is most visible at the level of the physical. No amount of zoom, no quality of scan, no 3D model captures what the conservator handles: the weight of paper, the texture of aging, the smell of old ink and accumulated time, the physical logic of something that has been folded, stored, carried, lost, and found.

This is not a critique of the project — it is a constraint that should be acknowledged rather than evaded. The site's rendering strategy (`docs/rendering-strategy.md`) is, at its core, a response to this constraint: use the simplest representation method that preserves the intended feeling, and be honest about what each level of representation can and cannot claim.

The appropriate response to the gap between representation and presence is not to simulate presence more convincingly, but to be specific about what is actually being preserved. A full `context_note` that records the occasion, the feeling, the reason for keeping — this carries more archival value than a more realistic scan. The written record and the material record are not substitutes for each other; they are different kinds of evidence, and a personal digital archive should offer both, clearly distinguished.

What this site can do that the institutional archive cannot is provide a seamless associative structure: a ticket linked to a film log entry linked to a sketch linked to a coffee log entry from the same day. The relational metadata (`related_ids`, `linked_event`, `companion_to`) and the eventual browse-by-event or browse-by-place functionality are not features the institutional archive typically offers for a collection this intimate and small. The digital archive compensates for its loss of materiality partly through relational density — the ability to move through a life by association rather than by category.

## The Self-Archiving Problem

The institutional archivist is separate from the fonds. The personal archivist is the fonds. This is the structural difference that makes self-archiving both more immediate and more compromised as an archival act. Institutional archives aspire to impartiality: Jenkinson's archivist "preserves the impartiality which is the chief characteristic of archival material." The personal archivist, archiving their own life, cannot be impartial. Every decision about what to keep, how to describe it, which items receive full records and which receive lightweight logs, is motivated — by affect, by self-image, by what the archivist wants to be true about themselves.

This is worth naming openly. The two record depths in `docs/content-model.md` — the lightweight log and the full archival record — will inevitably be applied inconsistently, and the inconsistency is evidence. Items that receive full treatment are items the archivist thought mattered. Items that remain lightweight logs are items that were important enough to record but not to fully interpret. Items that were never entered at all — the ones that were not kept — are the absent record of what the self chose not to preserve. An honest personal archive makes space for this incompleteness, names what is missing where possible, and does not pretend to be a total record of a life.

The archive guide, which this project defers in its full form to a later phase (`docs/roadmap.md`), will eventually need to address this directly. The most valuable thing a personal archive can offer a future reader is not completeness, but legibility: a clear account of what the archive contains, what it does not contain, and why those choices were made. The finding aid for a personal archive is, in some sense, a memoir of archival intention.
