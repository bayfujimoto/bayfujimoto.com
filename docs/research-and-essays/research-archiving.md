# On Archiving: Principles, Practices, and the Problem of the Kept Thing

*Research notes and essay — compiled April 2026*

---

## What Survives and Why

To archive is to make a claim about the future. Every kept thing is also an argument: this matters. This should remain. Someone who comes after should be able to find this, hold it, understand something from it. The archive is therefore never neutral — it is always already a record of a decision, and the decision is always political, personal, and contingent. What survives is not what was most important. It is what someone chose to keep, what the climate did not destroy, what the acid in the paper has not yet fully consumed, what no one thought to throw away.

The word "archive" descends from the Greek *arkheion* — the house of the *archons*, those who commanded. Derrida noticed this etymology in his 1995 lecture *Archive Fever*. The archive names, simultaneously, the commencement and the commandment: the place where things begin and the power that governs their arrangement. The archons were not just custodians; they held the authority to interpret, to make accessible or inaccessible, to say what counted as a record and what did not. Their house was both a storage facility and a jurisdiction. This double meaning has never dissolved. Every archive — institutional or personal, physical or digital, formal or private — retains both senses of the word. To archive something is to bring it under a kind of authority.

Derrida's argument is darker than it first appears. The archive does not simply store memory — it structures and threatens it. The "archive fever" he describes is the anxious, feverish desire to record everything, to leave no trace unpreserved, and it contains within itself a destructive impulse: in selecting what to keep, you necessarily destroy, or at least abandon, everything you did not select. The archive is constitutively incomplete. Every finding aid is also, implicitly, a list of what is not there.

This is the condition any serious archive must reckon with. Not as a problem to be solved — it cannot be solved — but as a constraint to be understood and, if possible, turned to use.

## The Intellectual Tradition: Provenance, Original Order, Appraisal

Modern archival theory crystallized in the late nineteenth century, when the sheer volume of state records produced by bureaucratic governments made ad hoc collection impossible. The Dutch Manual of 1898, produced by Samuel Müller, Johan Feith, and Robert Fruin, articulated what would become foundational: the principle of *respect des fonds*, which holds that records should be kept together according to their provenance — their point of origin — and never intermingled with records from other sources. A collection of papers from a ministry of trade should not be merged, reorganized, or selectively grafted with papers from a ministry of finance, even if the subject matter overlaps. The integrity of the fonds — the organic whole created by a particular institution, person, or activity — must be respected.

Closely related is *original order*: the idea that records should, where possible, be maintained in the sequence established by their creator. The order in which a person organized their correspondence, or in which a department filed its reports, is itself evidence. To reorganize by subject or date or perceived importance would be to impose a retrospective logic that was never part of the material's creation. The arrangement is the message.

These principles were challenged and extended by T.R. Schellenberg, the American archivist whose postwar work introduced a more active model of *appraisal* — the deliberate evaluation of records for long-term retention. Where Sir Hilary Jenkinson had argued that archivists should act as impartial custodians, leaving appraisal to record creators and resisting any act that would compromise the archive's "precious characteristic of impartiality," Schellenberg argued that appraisal was unavoidable and necessary. Not everything can be kept. The archivist must evaluate records for their primary value (the administrative, legal, or operational purposes for which they were created) and their secondary value (their evidential and informational usefulness for future researchers). Schellenberg's criteria — uniqueness, form, significance — opened the archive to active curatorial judgment.

This debate between Jenkinson and Schellenberg maps onto a tension that every archivist, personal or institutional, must navigate: the tension between completeness and selectivity. Jenkinson's vision preserves the organic whole but risks drowning in everything. Schellenberg's vision is more practical but introduces the risk of retrospective distortion, of organizing the archive according to what we think matters now rather than what the records themselves say.

Neither position is correct. Both are necessary orientations.

The Canadian archivist Terry Cook extended the debate into postmodern territory in the 1980s and 1990s, arguing that all appraisal is ideological and that archivists should recognize themselves as active agents in the construction of collective memory rather than passive custodians of a naturally forming record. Cook's concept of *macro-appraisal* focused appraisal decisions on the societal functions that generated records, rather than on the records themselves — asking not "is this document important?" but "is this activity important, and are these records evidence of it?" His work intersects with feminist and postcolonial critiques of the archive that ask whose records are kept, whose are excluded, and who gets to be represented in the official record of a life or a culture.

The international standard ISAD(G) — *General International Standard Archival Description*, published by the International Council on Archives — translates these theoretical commitments into practice. It defines four levels of description: the fonds, the series, the file, and the item. At each level, there are prescribed elements: reference code, title, dates, level of description, extent and medium, name of creator, scope and content, arrangement. The standard is hierarchical and cumulative: description at the fonds level applies to everything within it; description at the item level captures what is particular to a single record. This multi-level description is not merely bureaucratic. It is a philosophy of context: no item should be understood in isolation from the collection that contains it, and the collection cannot be understood without knowing something about the activity or life from which it came.

## Physical Archiving: The Body of the Record

Physical records degrade. Paper is acidic, and acidity is its slow death sentence. The lignin in wood-pulp paper — the ordinary paper of most of the twentieth century — oxidizes over time, yellowing and becoming brittle. Alum-rosin sizing, used to make paper smooth and writable, accelerates this decay. A newspaper from 1950 may already be too fragile to handle. A letter written on rag paper in 1750 may be in better condition.

Deacidification is the process by which free acids in paper are neutralized and an alkaline reserve is deposited to buffer against future acidic attack. It can be applied through aqueous methods (washing in alkaline solutions), non-aqueous solvents, or vapor-phase treatments. It does not restore brittle paper; it arrests its deterioration. A deacidified document still needs proper housing.

Encapsulation — placing a flat document between two sheets of transparent polyester film sealed at the edges — provides physical support and protects against atmospheric pollutants, handling damage, and moisture without adhering to the document in any way. The document can be removed by cutting the seal. It is a reversible treatment, which matters: reversibility is a core principle of archival conservation. Whatever you do to a record should, ideally, be undoable.

Housing is often as important as any chemical treatment. Acid-free folders and boxes, made from pH-neutral or alkaline-buffered board, provide an ongoing buffer against acid migration from adjacent materials. Flat storage is preferred for oversize items; rolled storage distorts paper over time. Photographs require separate treatment — the emulsions used in different photographic processes have different vulnerabilities, and interleaving, housing by type, and cold storage are variously recommended depending on the process.

Temperature and relative humidity are the twin enemies of physical collections. The ideal archival environment is cool (around 60°F / 15°C for mixed collections, colder for photographs and audiovisual materials) and relatively dry (around 30-40% RH), with minimal fluctuation — fluctuation is often more damaging than stable suboptimal conditions. Light, especially ultraviolet light, catalyzes degradation; archival storage should be dark, or at minimum filtered.

The finding aid is the intellectual counterpart to these physical interventions. A finding aid is a document that describes a collection: its provenance, its extent, its arrangement, its contents. It functions as a map that tells a researcher what exists, how it is organized, and where to look. At the collection level, a finding aid might describe the life and work of the person who created the papers; at the series level, it might describe the function that generated the records; at the folder or item level, it might note particular significant documents. The finding aid does not replace the records — it orients the researcher who approaches them.

Finding aids are themselves documents. They are interpretive acts. The language used to describe a collection, the decisions about what warrants individual description versus what can be bundled into a folder-level note, the choice to include biographical background or omit it — all of these are judgments that shape how future researchers encounter the material. A good finding aid is specific without being exhaustive, contextual without being editorial. The voice of the archivist is present in the finding aid even when it attempts to be absent.

Ephemera occupy a particular and interesting position in physical archival practice. By definition, ephemera are materials made not to last: tickets, receipts, menus, brochures, flyers, programs, trade cards, calling cards, handbills. They were produced cheaply, intended to be used once and discarded. The word comes from the Greek *ephemeron* — lasting only a day. Their survival is therefore always already an accident or an act of deliberate counter-preservation. Someone, against the logic of the object, kept it.

This is what makes ephemera so evocative as archival material. They were never meant to accumulate significance. They carried it inadvertently: in the printing style of their era, in the prices they recorded, in the events they admitted someone to, in the bodies that touched them and the pockets or drawers or shoeboxes that held them. A museum brochure from 1989 is not a significant document. But it is evidence of a visit, of an attention, of an economy of leisure, of a typographic convention, of a cultural relationship with institutions of culture. When a collection of such objects survives together — when the provenance of a personal archive can be traced — each piece reads differently than it would in isolation.

## Digital Preservation: The Problem of Format Rot

Digital materials are not stable. This is the central problem of digital preservation, and it is structurally different from the problem of paper degradation. Paper is a physical substrate that deteriorates slowly; given appropriate conditions, it can last centuries. Digital information depends on a chain of hardware, software, file formats, encoding standards, and operating environments, any link of which may break, become obsolete, or simply cease to be supported. A perfectly preserved floppy disk is useless if no working floppy drive exists to read it. A perfectly readable file is useless if no software remains to interpret its format. The container can survive while the content becomes inaccessible.

The Open Archival Information System (OAIS), formalized as ISO 14721, is the dominant reference model for digital preservation. It describes the full lifecycle of a digital object in an archival system: ingest, archival storage, data management, access, and administration. Crucially, it introduces a vocabulary of information packages — the Submission Information Package (SIP), the Archival Information Package (AIP), and the Dissemination Information Package (DIP) — that separate the object as submitted, the object as stored for preservation, and the object as delivered to an end user. These may differ in format, compression, and metadata. The model recognizes that preservation is not simply storage; it is ongoing curation.

Preservation metadata — data about data — is essential to this process. PREMIS (Preservation Metadata: Implementation Strategies) defines a core set of preservation metadata elements, covering intellectual entities, objects, events, agents, and rights. It records not just what an object is, but what has been done to it: what format migration, what authenticity check, what fixity verification. The metadata is the record of the object's archival life.

For description and discovery, Encoded Archival Description (EAD) provides an XML encoding standard for finding aids, allowing archival descriptions to be machine-readable and interoperable across institutions. Dublin Core provides a minimal, widely-adopted metadata element set — fifteen elements including title, creator, subject, date, format, and identifier — designed for broad interoperability across disciplines and contexts. Dublin Core is intentionally minimal; it can coexist with richer standards and provides a common floor beneath more specialized schemes.

Migration and emulation are the two dominant strategies for long-term access. Migration involves converting digital objects from obsolete or at-risk formats into formats more likely to remain accessible: a WordPerfect document migrated to plain text or PDF; a TIFF image preserved instead of a proprietary raw format. Migration preserves access at the cost of some transformation — each migration introduces the possibility of error or loss, however minor. Emulation involves building software environments that can run obsolete programs, preserving not just the data but the full rendering context. Emulation is more complex and resource-intensive, but it is the only strategy that genuinely preserves the experience of certain kinds of digital objects, particularly software, games, and interactive media.

The Library of Congress has articulated a set of recommended file formats for long-term preservation that prioritize open, well-documented standards: TIFF for still images; FLAC for audio; PDF/A for documents; plain text encoded in UTF-8 for written material. The preference is for formats that are not dependent on proprietary software, that have extensive documentation, that are widely used in relevant communities, and whose specifications are openly available.

A remarkable investigation into the mutability of digital interfaces over time was undertaken by Julianne Nyhan and collaborators in a study of the historical presentation of digital humanities resources, published in *Digital Scholarship in the Humanities*. Using the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine — itself one of the most significant personal-scale digital preservation projects ever undertaken — they traced how the interfaces of a sample of long-running digital humanities projects had changed, degraded, and sometimes disappeared entirely. The Wayback Machine preserves something like the surface of the web at irregular intervals: it captures pages as they were, including their visual design, their navigation, their embedded assets. But it captures imperfectly. Images go missing; stylesheets fail to load; interactive elements freeze. What remains is neither the original experience nor a stable document — it is a kind of degraded trace, an interface ephemeron. The study draws on frameworks from book history and interface design to argue that the visual and structural presentation of a digital resource is itself a form of historical evidence, subject to the same conditions of survival and loss as any other archival material.

## The Personal Archive: The Self as Archivist

Institutional archives are organized around the principle of custody: an institution takes responsibility for records it did not create, preserving them on behalf of a public that may not yet know it needs them. Personal archives work differently. The self-archiving individual is simultaneously the record creator, the archivist, and the anticipated future reader. The fonds and the archivist are the same person.

Susan Stewart's *On Longing* (1984) offers one of the most productive theoretical frameworks for thinking about this. Stewart distinguishes between two modes of object-oriented collecting: the souvenir, which is metonymic (a part that stands for a lost whole, always pointing backward toward an experience that cannot be recovered), and the collection, which is syntagmatic (a set whose meaning arises from its internal arrangement, not from any single external referent). The souvenir is nostalgic and personal; its meaning is biographical, indexical, irreplaceable. The collection is systematic and complete-in-itself; its meaning is generated by the set's internal relations.

Personal archives are typically neither pure souvenir nor pure collection — they are hybrid, unstable accumulations in which private nostalgia and systematic organization are in ongoing negotiation. A shoebox of ticket stubs is closer to the souvenir: each item points back to a specific night, a specific person, a specific version of the self that was there. A film log in a notebook is closer to the collection: it aspires to completeness, to the ability to say *I have watched these things*, to a record rather than a relic. The personal archive oscillates between these modes, and much of its psychological complexity arises from this oscillation.

The impulse to archive a life is, in part, a response to the problem Derrida named: the terror of loss, the awareness that memory is unreliable and time is erosive. But it is also something more than anxiety. It is a practice of attention. To keep a receipt, to note the coffee and the date, to scan a brochure — these acts train a particular kind of noticing. The archive is not only a storage system; it is a discipline of observation. The archivist learns to see things as potentially significant, to notice what is forgettable, to resist the default setting of discarding.

There is an important distinction between the *archive* and the *memory*. Memory is reconstructive, emotional, selective in ways we do not fully control. The archive is resistant to this retroactive revision. A note written in 2012 cannot be changed by what happened in 2019. The scan of a brochure carries exactly what it carried when it was saved. This is what archivists mean when they speak of the *evidential value* of records: not that the record is more true than memory, but that it is differently true — fixed, dateable, material, unrevised.

Community and grassroots archives have demonstrated that the personal archival impulse is not trivial or eccentric. Archives created by families, neighborhood organizations, activist communities, and individuals outside institutional frameworks preserve exactly the kind of material that official archives historically excluded: the everyday, the marginal, the un-heroic, the quietly continuous. They document lives that were not thought to be historically significant by the standards of the state or the academy. Their existence is an argument about what counts as evidence and who gets to be remembered.

## Principles That Carry Over

From this survey, a set of principles emerge that are applicable beyond the institutional archival setting — to personal projects, digital archives, and the kind of website this research was compiled to inform.

**Provenance matters more than subject.** Things should be kept together because of where they came from, not reorganized according to subject categories that were imposed afterward. A film ticket and a coffee bag from the same night belong together not because they share a topic but because they share an origin — a specific evening, a specific life.

**Original order is evidence.** The sequence in which things accumulate, the disorder of a desk, the chronological layering of a drawer — these are not problems to be corrected. They are data. Reorganization should be deliberate and acknowledged, not reflexive.

**Description is not neutral.** Every finding aid, every caption, every metadata field is an interpretive act. The language used to describe a record shapes how it will be found and read. Specificity is better than generality. "Admission ticket, MoMA, 12 March 2025" is better than "ticket." "Coffee brewed, Onyx Origins Washed Ethiopia, 4 January 2026" is better than "coffee."

**Levels of description enable scale.** Not everything needs to be fully described immediately. A lightweight record — an ID, a date, a type — is vastly better than nothing. Full records can be built over time. The minimum viable metadata is whatever is needed to prevent an item from becoming unidentifiable.

**Ephemera require deliberate treatment.** Items that were never meant to last require conscious decisions about housing, description, and inclusion. The decision to keep them is already an archival act; the decision about how to describe and present them determines whether they carry their evidential weight.

**Digital objects are formats as well as content.** A digital scan, a text file, a video — each exists in a specific format that encodes assumptions about software, hardware, and rendering. Preservation requires choosing formats for longevity (open, well-documented, widely supported) and maintaining the information needed to understand and migrate them over time.

**The interface is archival evidence.** The way a collection is presented — its visual language, its navigation, its metadata schema, its access model — becomes part of the archive's historical record. Interfaces change and degrade; this is itself worth documenting.

**Selection is unavoidable; acknowledge it.** Every archive is partial. The decision to include some things and not others is not a failure — it is the nature of archiving. The archivist's job is to make those decisions explicitly, to record them where possible, and to remain honest about what is not there.
